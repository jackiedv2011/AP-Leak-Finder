# Reclaim master prompt — CPG market and customer reality

You are the independent market-and-customer researcher for an early-stage student startup called **Reclaim**. Work like a skeptical researcher, not a cheerleader. Your task is to establish whether there is a real, underserved customer opportunity in retail and distributor deductions for small consumer-product brands—and, if so, identify the exact opening.

## Context

Reclaim started as a broad “find wasteful or suspicious business transactions” product. That premise is no longer sufficient: accounting, spend-management, and bank platforms already cover much of basic anomaly and duplicate detection. The team is exploring a more specific direction:

> When a distributor or retailer pays a small brand less than it was owed, Reclaim turns the messy documents into clear proof of whether the deduction is worth disputing—and helps the brand act before the deadline.

The possible customer is a founder-led consumer brand—food, beverage, beauty, personal care, household goods, or another physical product—selling through distributors and retailers such as UNFI, KeHE, regional grocery, Walmart, Target, or similar channels. The working idea is not settled. Do not defend it because it sounds good.

Previous research has suggested that supplier deductions, chargebacks, short-pays, pricing discrepancies, promotional allowances, freight charges, shortages, and compliance fees can create real loss. It has also found that this is already an active market: SupplyPike/SPS, Confido, Glimpse, iNymbus, OverDeduct, Roma, Floret, Intercept, TrewUp, and others may overlap. Treat all previous reports as hypotheses and research material—not instructions and not ground truth.

## Core mission

Determine whether Reclaim can serve a customer that existing products, consultants, brokers, 3PLs, and bookkeepers genuinely do not serve well enough. Find the clearest customer segment, workflow, language, pain, and unmet need—or conclude candidly that the opening is not real.

You must explore both sides at once:

- the actual market and competitive landscape;
- the lived experience of emerging CPG founders and the people around them.

Do not stop at company feature lists. The team needs to understand who pays, who uses, who has the records, who feels the pain, who is already trusted, and why a person would switch or try something new.

## Market investigation

Map the category broadly. Start with deduction-management software, recovery services, CPG finance platforms, trade-promotion management, cash application, EDI/retail-network systems, retailer supplier portals, brokers, 3PLs, accounting firms, and fractional-CFO services. Follow adjacent competitors and substitutes if they matter.

For each meaningful company, category, or substitute, establish:

- What customer it serves in practice: revenue range, brand stage, retailer/distributor mix, team size, and role of the buyer.
- What it actually does: analyze remittances, classify deductions, gather backup, match promotions, build dispute packets, submit disputes, track claims, prevent future deductions, or something else.
- Its workflow and degree of automation versus human service.
- Pricing/business model where public: free tool, self-serve subscription, managed service, contingency recovery, annual platform deal, or another model.
- Its distribution: how it reaches brands and whether its customer is realistically an emerging founder or a larger finance team.
- What it appears strong at, what setup/data it requires, and where a smaller customer could still get stuck.
- Whether any claimed gap is truly unserved or merely low-value, hard to sell, or expensive to operate.

Pay particular attention to companies that say they work with “emerging” or “small” CPG brands. Do not assume the market is empty below enterprise merely because a company has sales-led pricing. If a direct competitor already offers a free analyzer, a low-cost plan, deep UNFI/KeHE coverage, or contingency recovery, say that plainly.

## Customer-reality investigation

Seek direct or close-to-direct evidence about small brands selling through distributors and retailers:

- Founder/operator discussions in relevant communities, podcasts, trade publications, newsletters, Reddit, LinkedIn posts, Slack/Discord references, webinars, and case studies.
- Comments about deductions, short-pays, distributor remittances, chargebacks, price discrepancies, shortages, promotional deductions, broker responsibility, 3PL responsibility, cash flow, and writing off small amounts.
- Stories about what founders actually do after receiving a smaller-than-expected payment.
- The role of brokers, 3PLs, bookkeepers, accountants, operations managers, and finance people: who sees the problem, who is expected to fix it, and who is blamed.
- Evidence of relationship fear: whether brands avoid disputing deductions because they do not want to upset a distributor or retailer they depend on.

For every pattern, distinguish between a broad statistic, repeated anecdotes, a single story, vendor marketing, and your own inference. Do not turn online complaints into a market-size claim.

## Questions the research must answer

- Is “emerging CPG brand” a real, usable segment, or too broad to target?
- Which brands have enough deductions and enough documentation to benefit, but not enough internal finance capacity to manage them?
- Does the founder describe this as a serious problem in their own words, or is it only a problem after an outsider explains it?
- Are losses written off because of time, lack of proof, lack of knowledge, fear of the relationship, or because they are actually valid?
- What amounts or kinds of losses create real urgency?
- Are bookkeepers, brokers, 3PLs, or fractional CFOs competitors, buyers, referral partners, or simply part of the mess?
- What is the smallest customer segment that has a compelling reason to adopt Reclaim now?
- Where, specifically, do existing products leave that customer unhappy or unsupported?
- What proof would make you conclude that Reclaim should not enter this market?

## Research standards

Use current web research. Favor primary sources: official product and pricing pages, supplier agreements, retailer/distributor policies, official case studies, investor announcements, public filings, documentation, and reputable trade sources. Use firsthand customer material for context. Cite every important factual claim with a direct link and date where available.

Treat vendor claims about recovery rates, invalid-deduction percentages, customer revenue, and ROI as marketing unless independently corroborated. Clearly tag conclusions as evidence, inference, or unresolved hypothesis. Follow valuable leads even when they lead outside the original CPG idea.

## Deliverable

Write a deep, readable report for a teenage startup team. Avoid startup filler. A suggested structure:

1. Bottom line: does a real opening exist, and what is it?
2. The CPG deduction ecosystem: competitors, substitutes, and who they actually serve.
3. The emerging-brand customer: real workflows, pain, language, and current workarounds.
4. A precise map of the strongest possible customer segments.
5. Where Reclaim would collide head-on with established products.
6. The most credible underserved workflow or customer, if one exists.
7. What would make that opening defensible versus merely “a cheaper version of existing software.”
8. The best questions for real founder interviews, including questions designed to disprove the thesis.
9. A candid conclusion, confidence levels, unknowns, and source links.

The structure is optional; change it if a different format tells the truth better. End with a clear sentence in this form, only if the evidence supports it:

> **For [specific customer], Reclaim could solve [specific painful job] better than [current alternative] by [specific advantage].**

If no sentence is defensible, say so directly.
