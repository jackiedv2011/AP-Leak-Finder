# Reclaim final research master prompt — Market, business model, and kill test

You are the independent strategist assigned to decide whether Reclaim’s new direction is a real company or an attractive-sounding feature in an already crowded market. You must investigate the opportunity, the competition, customer value, pricing, sales path, and long-term potential—then try to kill the thesis with the strongest evidence you can find.

## Working direction

Reclaim is an early-stage student startup. Its broad original idea—detecting unusual, duplicate, or wasteful small-business transactions—has been heavily challenged because the detection layer is already bundled by financial and accounting incumbents.

The new direction under consideration is:

> **Reclaim helps small consumer brands selling through distributors and retailers understand and challenge invalid deductions or short-pays by matching what they were promised against what they were paid, organizing proof, and helping them act before a deadline.**

This may involve CPG brands in food, beverage, beauty, personal care, household goods, or another relevant supplier segment. The team is open to changing the segment, product, business model, or even abandoning this direction if the evidence says it should.

## Core mission

Answer the question a skeptical judge, customer, or investor would ask:

> **Why would a small brand choose Reclaim instead of existing deduction software, a recovery service, its broker, 3PL, accountant, spreadsheet, or simply doing nothing?**

Then test whether any answer survives.

## Market and competition

Map the real competitive market, including direct companies such as SupplyPike/SPS Revenue Recovery, Confido, Glimpse, iNymbus, OverDeduct, Roma, Floret, Intercept, TrewUp, as well as CPG ERPs, trade-promotion systems, cash-application tools, EDI providers, recovery consultants, brokers, 3PLs, accountants, and fractional CFOs. Find other relevant competitors rather than treating this list as complete.

For each serious player/category, determine:

- actual target customer and likely minimum scale;
- product scope and workflow;
- onboarding/data/integration burden;
- whether it is self-serve, managed, contingency-based, sales-led, or a broader platform;
- public pricing or pricing signals;
- customer claims and credible customer criticism;
- whether it truly serves early/founder-led brands or merely markets to them;
- how a small entrant might avoid a head-on feature race.

Be especially skeptical of the phrase “underserved downmarket.” A segment can be ignored because it is an opportunity—or because it cannot support the cost of selling and servicing it. Determine which is more likely.

## Customer economics and pricing

Build realistic scenarios for candidate customer segments. Do not use enterprise value figures without adjusting for actual smaller-brand volume and workflow.

Investigate:

- likely deduction volume, frequency, and disputable/recoverable dollars;
- what amount is meaningful to a founder-led brand;
- whether the value is recurring or a one-time cleanup;
- customer acquisition and onboarding costs implied by each model;
- subscription, per-case, flat-fee, contingency, hybrid, and partner/channel models;
- how value would be verified and billed fairly;
- whether contingency fees feel compelling, too expensive, too legally messy, or too labor-intensive;
- whether users would pay before seeing recovered dollars;
- what happens to retention when there are no deductions or when the product prevents them.

State your assumptions and show what would happen if each major assumption is wrong.

## Distribution and trust

Research how Reclaim could reach users without relying on an unrealistic sales machine. Explore founder-to-founder outreach, CPG communities, accelerators, trade shows, distributors, bookkeepers, fractional CFOs, brokers, 3PLs, CPG consultants, and partnerships.

For each route, ask:

- Why would the channel introduce Reclaim rather than do the work itself?
- Does it create trust or does it create dependency?
- Who owns the data and the relationship?
- What proof would a brand need before uploading financial documents or paying?
- Does the channel reach enough of the right customer type to become repeatable?

## The full kill test

Make the strongest non-strawman case that this version of Reclaim should fail. Investigate and judge objections such as:

- Existing tools already work for emerging brands, including in UNFI/KeHE and retailer-deduction workflows.
- The real problem is too low-value at the target customer size.
- The customer’s broker, 3PL, accountant, or finance person already handles it.
- The brand does not dispute because it fears harming a retailer/distributor relationship, not because it lacks a tool.
- Claims require difficult judgment, incomplete data, and human expertise that software cannot reliably replace.
- The first product is a feature competitors can copy immediately.
- Customer trust, document access, and portal restrictions stop adoption.
- Retailers/distributors can change their rules or automate the opportunity away.
- A student team can validate a workflow but cannot earn the trust needed for a durable company.

Seek actual evidence for each. Then identify only the narrowest, most defensible reasons the thesis might survive.

## Research standards

Use current, linkable evidence. Favor primary sources, official pricing/product pages, investor announcements, terms, partner documentation, credible customer material, and reliable business reporting. Treat competitor marketing and recovery-rate claims as leads rather than proof.

Clearly label evidence, inference, and unanswered questions. Do not offer legal advice; call out where qualified advice or adult partners would be needed.

## Deliverable

Write a hard, useful business report for a teenage startup team. Suggested structure:

1. The direct verdict: pursue, test only, change direction, or stop.
2. The strongest possible version of the business.
3. Competitive reality and what Reclaim cannot honestly claim.
4. Customer economics and pricing scenarios.
5. Distribution and trust strategy.
6. The hostile case against the business.
7. What survives the hostile case, if anything.
8. A believable growth path—or an explanation of why expansion is wishful thinking.
9. Exact validation gates before building heavily.
10. Sources, confidence levels, major assumptions, and unknowns.

Use another format if it is more truthful or clear. End with an unambiguous conclusion:

> **Reclaim should / should not begin as [specific product] for [specific customer], sold through [specific path], priced as [specific model], because [evidence-based reason].**

If the evidence does not support a credible answer, say that plainly and name the next-best direction to investigate.
