# Reclaim final research master prompt — Claims, evidence, and real workflow

You are conducting a forensic, evidence-first investigation for an early-stage student startup called **Reclaim**. This is a fresh research run. Do not inherit the conclusions of prior research just because they were written confidently; use previous reports only as leads to verify, challenge, improve, or reject.

## The working Reclaim direction

Reclaim originally aimed to find wasteful, duplicate, or suspicious financial transactions for small businesses. The team learned that broad detection is weak: banks, accounting software, spend platforms, and finance tools already perform many basic detection tasks, often as bundled features.

The team is now exploring a much sharper possible thesis:

> **When a small consumer brand is paid less than it was promised by a distributor or retailer, Reclaim helps turn the brand’s scattered records into clear evidence of whether the deduction is worth challenging, before the right to challenge it expires.**

The customer might be a founder-led food, beverage, beauty, personal-care, home-goods, or similar CPG brand selling through distributors and retailers such as UNFI, KeHE, regional grocery, Walmart, Target, or another channel discovered in your research.

Reclaim is not committed to this thesis. Your job is to discover whether the actual mechanics support it, whether a stronger initial claim type or customer exists, and where the proposed workflow breaks.

## Core mission

Trace real deduction-recovery workflows all the way through—from a brand being paid short, to identifying the reason, to proving whether it is valid, to submitting the challenge, to a credit or denial.

Do not stop at “deductions happen.” A product cannot help merely because a problem exists. It needs to know what occurred, what document proves it, who decides, how a case is filed, what time is available, and what outcome follows.

## Research questions

### 1. Which customer lane is actually workable?

Explore the relevant landscape broadly: UNFI, KeHE, other grocery distributors, regional distributors, retailers, DSD, 1P supplier relationships, wholesale marketplaces, or adjacent verticals with a better match. Do not assume the names above are the answer.

For each serious lane, identify:

- Who the supplier/customer is and what size/stage of company can realistically participate.
- How the supplier is paid and where short-pays or deductions show up.
- Whether the supplier receives data via email, EDI, portal, remittance advice, ERP, accounting system, broker, 3PL, or another channel.
- The main deduction categories and which ones are likely valid versus meaningfully disputable.
- Deadlines, claim channels, evidence requirements, decision-makers, appeal paths, and resolution mechanics.
- What changes between a small founder-led brand, a growing brand with an operations manager, and an enterprise supplier.

### 2. What is the best first claim type?

Investigate at least several candidate claim types, including but not limited to:

- short/over/incorrect receiving or shipment shortage deductions;
- price, cost, or invoice discrepancies;
- duplicate promotional, trade-spend, or allowance deductions;
- freight, backhaul, or carrier-related allowances;
- retailer compliance or routing-guide chargebacks;
- returns, damages, unsaleables, or slotting/new-item fees;
- any better category you uncover.

For every candidate, explain:

- What must be true for the deduction to be invalid.
- What exact documents or fields establish that truth.
- Where those documents usually live and whether a small brand can obtain them.
- How much judgment is involved. Can a reliable product identify a supported case, or is the matter too ambiguous?
- Whether the dispute is recurring and valuable enough to matter.
- Whether the customer may avoid disputing for relationship reasons even when it has proof.

Do not recommend a claim type simply because it is common. Recommend it only if evidence is concrete, the process is workable, the customer can act, and false positives can be controlled.

### 3. What does “proof” really mean?

Build a detailed evidence map. Analyze records such as:

- remittance advice, deduction notice, payment/settlement detail, and deduction codes;
- purchase orders, invoices, price sheets, trade agreements, promotional authorizations, routing guides, and supplier agreements;
- bills of lading, proof of delivery, packing lists, carrier tracking, appointment records, ASN/EDI records, and 3PL documentation;
- credit memos, prior claims, emails, portal screenshots, and distributor backup.

For each document, identify useful fields, common mismatches, data quality problems, and the difference between a document that supports a claim and one that conclusively proves it.

Map a claim packet in plain language: what must be included, how the narrative should work, which documents are attachments, and what would cause the claim to fail.

### 4. What can Reclaim legitimately do?

Research account roles, portal restrictions, APIs, terms of service, official partner programs, document-export options, delegated access, and rules around third-party submission. Do not guess.

Separate these levels clearly:

1. Reclaim helps a brand understand a deduction from customer-uploaded files.
2. Reclaim prepares a packet, draft, checklist, or claim form and the brand submits it.
3. Reclaim operates alongside the brand while the brand controls its own portal.
4. Reclaim submits or manages claims as a third party.
5. Reclaim integrates directly or operates through a formal partnership.

For each, explain what is likely allowed, what is uncertain, what requires customer consent, and what should not be attempted without specialist legal/partner support.

## Research standards

Use current web research and primary sources wherever possible: supplier terms, distributor policies, retailer routing guides, portal documentation, official claim instructions, API/partner documentation, official price schedules, and credible case studies. Use vendor blogs only as leads; label them as marketing unless independently corroborated.

Every important factual claim should have a direct link and a date where available. Clearly separate:

- established evidence;
- reasonable inference;
- unresolved questions;
- claims that can only be tested with real supplier documents and interviews.

Follow unexpected leads. If a non-CPG workflow better satisfies the evidence-and-claim structure, elevate it rather than forcing a CPG answer.

## Deliverable

Produce a thorough, plain-English report for a teenage startup team. A suggested structure:

1. The honest bottom line: is this a real Reclaim workflow or not?
2. The best customer lane and why.
3. A comparison of claim types, evidence quality, deadlines, and risk.
4. A step-by-step map from short-pay to resolved claim.
5. The evidence packet: documents, fields, and where they come from.
6. What Reclaim can responsibly do now versus later.
7. The largest failure points and how to test them immediately.
8. The three most useful real-world case studies the team should seek.
9. Sources, confidence levels, unknowns, and disconfirming evidence.

The format is a guide, not a restriction. Use diagrams or tables if helpful, but prioritize truth and detail.

End with one decisive recommendation:

> **Reclaim should first help [specific kind of supplier] challenge [specific deduction type] using [specific records and workflow], because [why the evidence and claim path are unusually strong].**

If no version of that sentence survives the research, say so directly.
