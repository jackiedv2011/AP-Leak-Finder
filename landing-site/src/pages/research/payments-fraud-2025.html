Payments fraud in 2025

Sources checked Sep 2026 Survey AFP · FBI IC3
Accounts Payable Duplicate Payments Payment Mismatches Checks Email

SUMMARY

Two sources counted payments fraud in 2025 from opposite ends. The Association for Financial Professionals surveyed 465 treasury practitioners at US organizations and found 76% had faced attempted or actual payments fraud during the year. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center counted what victims reported to it: 1,008,597 complaints and $20.877 billion in losses, up 26% on 2024.

The two measure different things and should not be added together. AFP reports the share of organizations that experienced an attempt, whether or not money moved. IC3 reports money that people said they lost, in complaints they chose to file.

IC3 took about 3,000 complaints a day across every kind of internet crime, not only the ones aimed at companies.

Both put business email compromise near the top. AFP found 74% of organizations affected by it in 2025, a significant increase on 2023 and 2024. At IC3 it was the second-largest loss category of the year, behind investment fraud.

Neither figure tells you what happened at any one organization, and neither is a rate of loss. What they give you is a sense of how common the attempt has become, and which channels it comes through.

WHAT BEC ACTUALLY MEANS

The FBI's definition is narrower than the everyday use of the term:

  • It targets businesses and individuals who work with suppliers or regularly send wire transfers.
  • The attacker compromises email or another channel, through social engineering or intrusion, and uses it to authorize a transfer that looks legitimate.
  • It is not the same thing as a phishing email, though phishing is often how it starts.

In 2025 that came to $3,046,598,558 across 24,768 complaints, against $2,770,151,146 the year before. More than $30 million of it involved AI in some form.

FINDINGS

Checks are still the most attacked payment method. AFP's respondents named them far more often than anything else.

  • Checks 58%, ACH debits 30%, wire transfers 25%.
  • 72% of organizations that use checks plan to keep using them, and 68% of those say it is because vendors want them.

So the most attacked instrument is also the one nobody can drop, because the decision belongs to the supplier rather than to the payer.

Loss falls unevenly. 48% of organizations under $1 billion in revenue reported financial losses, against 66% above it.

Smaller organizations face fraud less often, but they are much more likely to absorb the whole amount when it happens.

Treasury is where it surfaces. It was the function most likely to discover both attempted fraud, at 83%, and actual fraud, at 55%, usually working alongside accounts payable.

Defences are further behind than the threat. Only 17% of organizations use AI to counter payments fraud at all; among those that do, 49% report working more efficiently, 45% use it to detect deepfakes and 43% for real-time monitoring.

IC3's own list puts investment fraud first by losses, then BEC, then tech support scams — a reminder that the money aimed at companies is one part of a much larger total.

TAKEAWAYS

Fraud and mistakes are different problems with the same paper trail. A vendor who changes bank details because they moved banks, and an attacker who changes them because they want the money, both leave the same record: a change to a payment instruction, followed by a payment that went somewhere new. Neither is visible from the payment itself. Both are visible from the records around it.

That is why these numbers are worth reading even if nobody suspects anything. The checks they imply are the same ones that catch the ordinary version.

It also helps to know which of these numbers describes you. AFP surveyed 465 treasury practitioners at US organizations; IC3 counted complaints from anyone who filed one. Neither sample is your company.

  • Vendor detail changes. Every change to bank details, remit-to address or contact email is a record with a date. Being able to list them is most of the work.
  • Check runs. Checks are the most attacked instrument and the hardest to retire. Matching every check to a bill, and every bill to one check, is the basic control.
  • Duplicate instructions. A payment run that goes out twice looks the same in the ledger whether the cause was a system error or somebody's second attempt.
  • The channel, not the amount. Attacks cluster by payment method rather than by size. Checks and email are where they concentrate, regardless of what the payment was for.
  • Who gets asked. Treasury finds most of it, usually with accounts payable. The two teams see different halves of the same record.
  • What you can answer quickly. AFP's respondents mostly found fraud through people rather than tooling. Shortening the time it takes to answer a question about a payment is worth more than it sounds.

THE BOTTOM LINE

Reclaim isn't a fraud tool. It reads exports of your bills, payments, vendors and credits and shows where they don't agree — the same records anyone would pull to answer a question about a payment, lined up so the answer takes minutes instead of an afternoon. What the discrepancy turns out to be is for you to decide.
