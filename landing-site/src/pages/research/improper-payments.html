$186 billion in improper payments

Sources checked Sep 2026 Report U.S. GAO
Overpayments Duplicate Payments Controls Public Sector Reporting

SUMMARY

The US Government Accountability Office reported about $186 billion in improper payments across 64 federal programs at 15 agencies in fiscal 2025. About $153 billion of that, roughly 82%, was overpayment: money that went out in a larger amount than it should have, or that should not have gone out at all. It is one of the few places where payments that went wrong are counted, published and audited year after year.

An improper payment, in GAO's definition, is one that should not have been made or that was made in an incorrect amount. That covers more than fraud. It includes payments to ineligible recipients, payments for services never received, duplicate payments, and payments where the documentation needed to verify them was missing.

The total rose about $24 billion from the $162 billion reported in fiscal 2024. GAO attributes most of the increase to programs that reported estimates in 2025 but not in 2024, together with higher improper payments in Medicaid.

Scale is the reason to read it. Almost no private organization publishes a number like this, and none publishes one built the same way two years running. The federal estimates are imperfect, but they are consistent and they are public.

WHERE THEY CONCENTRATE

The money is not spread evenly across the 64 programs:

  • 73% of the total sits in five program areas: Medicare, Medicaid, the Earned Income Tax Credit, SNAP and the Shuttered Venue Operators Grant.
  • 19 programs reported improper payment rates of at least 10%, and six reported rates above 25%.

A high rate and a large amount are not the same thing. A small program can report a rate above 25% and account for very little money; Medicare can report a modest rate and account for a great deal.

Cumulatively, GAO puts improper payments since fiscal 2003 at about $3 trillion, and says the actual amount may be much higher.

FINDINGS

The estimate is not the whole number. GAO is explicit that its figures do not represent the full extent of improper payments: some programs produce no estimate at all. Temporary Assistance for Needy Families is one.

Reporting quality is part of the story. Inspectors general found that half of the 24 agencies covered by the Payment Integrity Information Act fully complied with it in fiscal 2024.

  • Five agencies did not complete risk assessments for all of their programs.
  • Seven produced improper payment estimates that were not reliable.

GAO's recommendations follow from that: designate programs paying more than $100 million a year as susceptible to significant improper payments, and put internal control plans in place for emergency funding.

Of the ten matters it raised for congressional consideration in March 2022, nine were still open as of April 2026.

The causes GAO names for the Medicaid increase are ordinary administrative ones: eligibility redeterminations, provider screening, and COVID-era flexibilities being phased out. Nothing exotic.

This is not an argument that federal programs are careless. It is an argument that when payments are measured at this scale, this is what shows up.

Most of what makes a payment improper is a missing check, a stale record, or a step nobody completed.

TAKEAWAYS

Federal programs are not companies, and a Medicaid eligibility error has little in common with a duplicate freight invoice. The mechanics are different and so is the scale. What carries across is the shape of the finding: money that left, in the wrong amount, for reasons that were knowable at the time.

GAO's number is also unusual in being audited. Agency estimates are reviewed by inspectors general, and GAO says plainly where it thinks they fall short. Very little private benchmarking works that way.

  • Overpayment dominates. 82% of the total was money that went out when it shouldn't have, or in a larger amount than it should have. Underpayment and unverifiable documentation are the smaller share.
  • Volume concentrates. Five program areas hold 73% of the total. In most ledgers the same is true: a handful of vendors or payment types account for most of what goes wrong.
  • Rates and amounts are different questions. 19 programs reported rates above 10%, but rate tells you about process quality and amount tells you about exposure. Both are worth knowing; neither substitutes.
  • What isn't measured doesn't disappear. Programs without an estimate are not programs without improper payments. The same is true of a vendor nobody has reconciled.
  • Controls sit before the payment. Every recommendation in the report is about something done before money leaves, not about recovering it afterwards.
  • Risk assessment comes before detection. GAO's recommendations are mostly about knowing which programs to look at. The equivalent question is which of your vendors, accounts and payment types deserve attention first.

The cumulative figure is the one worth sitting with. About $3 trillion since fiscal 2003, and GAO's own view is that the real number may be much higher — because the estimate depends on programs measuring themselves.

THE BOTTOM LINE

Improper payments at this scale are an accounting problem before they are anything else: records that didn't agree, and checks that happened after the money moved rather than before.
