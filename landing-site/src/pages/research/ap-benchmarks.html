How often payments go out wrong

Sources checked Sep 2026 Benchmark APQC · Ardent Partners
Accounts Payable Duplicate Payments Duplicate Bills Payment Mismatches Benchmarks

SUMMARY

Two published benchmarks describe how often accounts payable gets a payment wrong. APQC puts duplicate or erroneous payments at 1.5% of disbursements at the median organization, across a sample of 1,686 companies. Ardent Partners puts the average invoice exception rate at 18.4%. Neither number is a loss figure, and neither describes a single company. That distinction matters more than the figures do.

APQC's measure counts disbursements, not dollars. A disbursement is a single remittance to a supplier, so the 1.5% describes how many payments in a year were duplicate or erroneous, not how much money they carried. The sample behind the median is 1,686 organizations.

Ardent's 18.4% is a different thing again: the share of invoices that need exception handling before they can be paid. An exception is a bill that stops, not a bill that was paid wrong. The two are related, though, because invoices that fall out of the normal flow get handled by hand, and hands are where duplicates come from.

Both are medians or averages taken across a large sample, which means half the organizations measured sit on either side of them.

WHAT THE NUMBERS LOOK LIKE IN PRACTICE

At the median rate, an organization issuing 4,000 payments a year would expect roughly 60 of them to be duplicate or erroneous. That is a count, not a cost:

  • A duplicate on a $400 freight bill and a duplicate on a $40,000 equipment invoice both count as one.
  • Sizing the money is a separate exercise from counting the payments, and the two rarely land on the same scale.

Ardent's cost benchmarks sit alongside it:

  • $9.84 is the average cost to process a single invoice, and 8.2 days is the average time it takes.
  • 57% of suppliers can send invoices electronically, which leaves a large share still arriving as paper or email attachments.

FINDINGS

Exceptions are the drag on every other number. Ardent describes them as the biggest single reason the benchmarks aren't better than they are: an invoice that stops has to be chased, and chasing is slow and expensive.

Some of what that chasing costs shows up in the supplier relationship:

  • 21.9% of accounts payable staff time goes on dealing with suppliers, which Ardent attributes in no small part to exceptions.
  • 39% of AP leaders say manual, low-value work is holding the function back.

Ardent defines Best-in-Class as the fifth of organizations with the lowest average invoice processing costs and the shortest cycle times. The gap between them and everyone else is wide:

  • Costs are 79% lower and processing is 79% faster.
  • Exception rates are 47% lower.
  • They have 1.4 times as many suppliers sending invoices electronically, and more than 1.8 times as many invoices going through without a person touching them.

APQC's measure sits inside a defined process scope that includes verifying the pay file against the vendor master, auditing invoices and key data, and researching and resolving payable exceptions. The same steps, in other words, that the exception rate describes.

TAKEAWAYS

Benchmarks are useful for scale, not for scoring. A rate published across 1,686 organizations tells you what order of magnitude to expect; it does not tell you what happened in your own ledger. These are the things worth doing with it.

The two measures also answer different questions. APQC asks how many payments went out wrong. Ardent asks how many invoices could not be processed cleanly. An organization can look good on one and poor on the other, and the gap between them is usually where the manual work lives.

  • Count first, then size. APQC's 1.5% is a count of payments. Before anyone asks what a duplicate rate costs, it helps to know how many payments are in question and which vendors they sit with.
  • Group exceptions by reason. An exception rate is an average across very different problems: a missing purchase order, a price that doesn't match, a vendor name spelled two ways. Each has a different fix.
  • Check the vendor master. APQC's process scope starts with verifying the pay file against the vendor master. Duplicate vendor records are one of the quiet ways the same invoice gets paid twice.
  • Watch where supplier time goes. If a fifth of the team's time is spent answering suppliers, the questions themselves are data. The vendors who ask most often are usually the ones whose invoices stop most often.
  • Read the Best-in-Class gap as a range, not a target. A 47% lower exception rate is what the top fifth achieve. The useful question is which of their practices are available to you now.

THE BOTTOM LINE

Neither benchmark is a loss figure, and neither is a finding. They describe how often payments and invoices go wrong across a large sample, which is a reason to look at your own records rather than a substitute for looking.
