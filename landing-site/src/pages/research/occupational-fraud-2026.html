What 2,402 fraud cases show

Sources checked Sep 2026 Report ACFE

SUMMARY

The ACFE's Occupational Fraud 2026: A Report to the Nations covers 2,402 real cases from 143 countries and territories, with total losses above $3.4 billion. Billing schemes — invoices for things never delivered, inflated invoices, or personal purchases run through the company — appear in 21% of them. The typical case ran 12 months before anyone caught it.

The report sorts fraud into three categories. Asset misappropriation is by far the most common, in 90% of cases, with a median loss of $100,000. Corruption appears in 45% of cases at $150,000. Financial statement fraud is the rarest, at 6% of cases, but the most expensive at $1 million.

Across all 2,402 cases the median loss was $104,000 and the average was $1,457,000. The gap between those two numbers is the shape of the problem: most cases are moderate, and a small number are very large.

Both are worth reading with the sample in mind. These are cases that were found, investigated and reported to a Certified Fraud Examiner. The report does not claim to measure fraud that nobody caught.

SCHEMES THAT RUN THROUGH PAYABLES

Two of the categories land squarely in accounts payable:

  • Billing schemes are 21% of all cases, with a median loss of $90,000 and a median duration of 14 months.
  • Check and payment tampering is rarer at 10% of cases, but it is the most costly form of asset misappropriation at $114,000, running a median of 13 months.

Both run longer than the 12-month overall median, which is what you would expect from schemes that look like ordinary invoices and ordinary payments.

Size matters too. Organizations with fewer than 100 employees had the highest median loss of any size band, at $126,000, and billing schemes made up 28% of their cases against 18% at larger organizations.

FINDINGS

Time is the variable that drives loss. The report groups cases by how long they ran before detection, and the curve is steep.

  • Frauds caught within the first six months, about a third of all cases, had a median loss of $40,000.
  • Another one to six months more than doubled that, to $100,000.
  • The 5% that ran more than five years had a median loss of $1,115,000.

Which makes how fraud gets found the second question. Tips lead by a distance, at 43% of cases — nearly three times the next method.

  • Internal audit found 15%, management review 13%, document examination and account reconciliation 5% each.
  • Automated transaction and data monitoring found 3%, and external audit 2%.
  • 4% were discovered by accident.

None of this is a comment on audit quality. External audits are not designed to find occupational fraud; the numbers reflect what each method is for.

The ACFE separates these into active methods, where someone goes looking, and passive ones, where the organization waits to be told. Frauds found by active methods can be uncovered up to four times faster.

More than half of all cases involved either a lack of internal controls or an override of the controls that existed.

TAKEAWAYS

This is a fraud report, and most of what goes wrong in accounts payable is not fraud. Someone keys an invoice twice, a vendor sends the same bill through two channels, a credit never gets applied. But the controls the ACFE measures are the same controls that catch honest mistakes, and the evidence behind them is unusually specific.

For every anti-fraud control it tracks, the report compares organizations that had it with organizations that did not. Three of them are ordinary bookkeeping practice.

  • Proactive data monitoring. In place at 49% of victim organizations. Median loss $70,000 with it against $150,000 without, and 9 months to detection against 16.
  • Management review. In place at 71%. Median loss $84,000 against $186,000, and 10 months against 18.
  • Surprise audits. In place at 44%. Median loss $74,000 against $149,000, and 8 months against 16.
  • Duration is the lever. Every control that lowered losses also shortened detection time, and the two move together. Nothing in the report separates them.
  • Smaller organizations are more exposed. Under 100 employees the median loss was $126,000, the highest of any size band, and only about a quarter had a reporting hotline against 85% of larger organizations.
  • Controls get fixed after the fact. 80% of victim organizations modified their controls once the fraud came to light.

The comparison is not a controlled experiment. Organizations that run surprise audits differ from those that don't in other ways too. But the direction is consistent across all eighteen controls the report tracks, and the size of the differences is hard to ignore.

Across all of them, having a control in place was associated with losses 37% lower on average. Where a lack of controls was the primary factor, median losses ran 43% higher.

THE BOTTOM LINE

Reclaim is not a fraud tool and doesn't try to be one. It reads the records you already have and points at the payments and bills that don't line up — the same records a reviewer would pull, in the same order. Whether what it finds is a mistake or something else is a question for the people who know the vendor.
